Data Centers Decoded

An investor's risk browser that links where AI data centers are being built, the policy pushing back on them, and the web of deals that finances them, so an analyst can see how a shock in one corner of the AI complex reaches every company exposed to it.

Katie Bays · Repo: github.com/katiebays/MGT409FINAL · App: runs locally from the repo (python app.py; see README) · Model: gpt-6-luna

The insight it surfaces

Alphabet looks insulated from the Texas fight, with only 12% of its own pipeline there. It is hyper-exposed to Kentucky instead: an 18-month pause there touches $130.0B of commitment-weighted deal value, almost all of it through Anthropic, whose disclosed capacity sits in Kentucky.

1. Motivation

Who has the problem. Project-finance lenders, debt syndicators and infrastructure investors have become some of the largest providers of capital to AI infrastructure. They underwrite data-center construction loans, GPU-backed facilities, lease and offtake-backed debt, and equity in developers and neoclouds. I work with these investors at my firm, and they are spending significant time trying to answer one question: what is my real exposure if something changes?

Why it is hard. The AI build-out runs on interlocking, often circular agreements. Chipmakers invest in labs and neoclouds that buy their chips; hyperscalers sign offtakes with neoclouds that lease from developers financed by bank syndicates; labs commit hundreds of billions in minimum spend. Two kinds of shock now hit this web at once:

  • Policy volatility. Local moratoria on data centers have proliferated (882 in the tracker used here, 715 currently in effect), alongside state bills, grid-queue pauses and cost-allocation rules. The midterm election added more uncertainty.
  • Counterparty and governance shocks. A single event at a hub company, such as NVIDIA's dismissal and later rehiring of Jensen Huang, ripples to every lab, neocloud and lender whose deals reference that company.

How people cope today. Analysts stitch together a site tracker, a bill tracker, company filings and deal-data terminals in spreadsheets. Each source is organized around its own object (a site, a bill, a company), so the cross-cutting question "a Texas moratorium hits which tenants, which lenders, which chipmaker backstops?" requires manual joins that consume significant analyst time and go stale as soon as a new deal or ordinance lands.

What it costs them. Missed concentration (a lender to a neocloud whose pipeline is 93% in one state), mispriced risk (treating a $300B "up to" headline as a firm commitment), and slow reaction when policy moves.

The new piece is the join: one entity registry links 2,925 sites, 201 interparty agreements and 942 policy records, so exposure can be traced directly (my own sites) and indirectly (my counterparties' sites and deals). Three design choices go beyond a dashboard:

  1. Red flags with three separate scores. Severity, likelihood and a confidence score that measures evidence quality and is reduced for each counterparty hop, so an extrapolated exposure is never presented as fact.
  2. Commitment weighting. Firm minimums count fully, "up to" ceilings at half, aspirational headlines at a quarter; re-announced deals count once. Naively summed, NVIDIA's deal book was $2.8T; weighted, it is $66B.
  3. An AI analyst that cannot invent numbers. The LLM narrates; every figure it states must come from a tool call, or the answer is rejected.

3. The agents

Solid boxes ship today; dashed boxes are designed and specified, not shipped.

Analyst agent (shipped)

Job: turn an investor's plain-English question ("I'm considering CoreWeave, where are the red flags?", "what if Texas freezes construction for two years?") into the right engine calls and a short, sourced answer with confidence labels.

ToolWhat it returns
get_red_flags(company)Top 10 flags across project delay, state policy, counterparty; each with severity, likelihood, risk, confidence + reasons, evidence IDs
run_scenario(company, states, months)MW delayed, extrapolated deferred revenue (assumption stated), deal value exposed via counterparties, with paths
get_counterparties(company)Weighted deal book by counterparty and group, HHI concentration, second-degree links
get_state_policies(state)Policy items plus moratorium counts and the 15 most recent active/proposed moratoria

Organization. One LLM agent sits on top of a deterministic engine. I deliberately kept the arithmetic out of the model: exposure math, scoring and site-to-moratorium matching are plain Python, so every number is reproducible and the agent's only job is choosing tools and explaining results. Two further agents are specified to keep the data alive (see diagram): a Policy Scout that searches LegiScan and news weekly and queues candidate restrictions for human review, and a Deal Extraction agent that reads filings and releases into structured edges. For this submission the data comes from my firm's feeds and an existing tracker I built, so I reused that tooling rather than rebuild it.

Model. gpt-6-luna. It is the course model, it handles multi-tool calls reliably in testing (10/10 benchmark turns completed), and at $0.10 / $0.50 per million input/output tokens it keeps a question under a tenth of a cent. The app tries the course Portkey gateway first and falls back to a direct OpenAI key (the Yale Portkey key expired after the term, so the measured runs below used a personal OpenAI key).

What cut cost, and whether it hurt

  • Deterministic engine. Picking a company from the dropdown renders the full report with zero LLM calls; only questions use the model.
  • Prompt caching. Follow-ups reuse the conversation prefix; 65% of input tokens were cached at one-tenth the price. Without caching the same turns would average $0.0011 per question (65% more).
  • Capped tool payloads. Flags capped at 10; state policy lists capped at counts plus 15 records (Michigan alone has 100 moratoria).
  • Payload trimming: measured, no effect. I A/B-tested sending trimmed flags vs. the full flag objects. After the 10-flag cap, the difference was within noise, and neither version had a grounding failure, so I kept trimming for safety rather than cost. Evidence (input / output tokens):

What is newly possible. Questions that used to require joining three tools by hand now answer in seconds, with the path shown:

  • A Texas construction freeze delays 93% of Crusoe's pipeline (7,273 MW) and 51% of CoreWeave's (2,324 MW); CoreWeave's Lancaster County, PA campus also sits near an active township moratorium.
  • OpenAI owns little in Texas but its largest counterparty, Oracle, is 53% of its weighted deal book; four Ohio bills sit on its 13,000 MW there.
  • Alphabet's Kentucky exposure through Anthropic ($130.0B) dwarfs its Texas exposure ($3.9B), the opposite of what a site map alone suggests.

Real user reaction. Before building this, we showed a project-finance investor a far less sophisticated cut of the same information: a table of CoreWeave's projects placed next to a separate list of policy items. This investor has underwritten a number of investments in these companies. Seeing the two side by side, they told us:

"I really feel like we're sitting on all of the dynamite when you look at it this way."
Project-finance investor with underwritten exposure to these companies, reacting to a static projects-plus-policy table

That reaction came from a static, unjoined view. Data Centers Decoded does the join automatically: it matches each project to the moratoria and bills that cover it, carries the exposure through to tenants, lenders and investors, and scores how much to trust each link.

7. Limits and next steps

  • Deal parsing is regex-based. Dollar amounts and firmness come from pattern matching; money direction (who pays whom) is not modeled. Next: the Deal Extraction agent, evaluated against a hand-labeled set of 50 agreements.
  • Site coverage. 1,374 of 2,865 active sites link to a tracked company; tenants are sparse (114 rows), so lab exposure leans on deals.
  • Policy freshness. The tracker export is a snapshot. Next: ship the Policy Scout (weekly LegiScan + news) with a human review queue.
  • Scenario economics. One $10M per MW-year revenue assumption; operating sites are excluded even though expansions may be exposed.
  • Governance shocks. The engine models geographic shocks; a "counterparty shock" scenario (e.g. NVIDIA leadership turmoil) would reuse the same graph and is next.
